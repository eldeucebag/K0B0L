# Storytelling

A story is not a sequence of events — it is a sequence of *changes*. The
distinction that matters: things happen in chronological order, but a
story runs on what those events do to a character. If a scene changes
nothing for anyone, it is a report.

## Before prose: the four questions

Answer these in a few lines before writing a word of the story itself:

1. **Who wants what, and what's in the way?** Desire + obstacle = plot.
   A character who wants nothing generates events, not story.
2. **What changes by the end?** Not what *happens* — what is *different*
   in the character or the world. If nothing is, you wrote an anecdote.
   Anecdotes are fine; call them that.
3. **Where does the reader enter and leave?** Open as close to the change
   as the story survives; leave before the explanation does.
4. **Why this telling, this length?** Flash fiction, short story, and
   novel answer the same question at different depths. The form is a
   container — pick it before filling it.

## Scene-level craft

- **Enter late, leave early.** Cut the scene's first paragraph and last
  paragraph; if nothing breaks, they were throat-clearing.
- **Ground the reader in a body, not a camera.** Sensation first —
  weather is only weather when a character feels it on their skin.
- **Dialogue is action.** People talk *to do things*, not to exchange
  information the reader already has. If a line only explains, cut it or
  make someone lie in it.
- **Detail is a budget.** Three specifics beat thirty; the reader
  finishes what a specific starts. A generic description spends the
  budget and buys nothing.
- **Tension is unanswered questions.** Every scene plants one the reader
  must turn the page to resolve. Resolve it just late enough that the
  next one is already planted.

## Revision as discipline

First drafts are for finding the story; the revision is where it becomes
one. Rework in this order — structure first, because fixing prose on a
broken structure is painting a house before the foundation cures:

1. Scene list, one line each: whose scene, what changes. Cut or merge
   the ones with no answer.
2. The spine: does want → obstacle → change actually run? Fix at this
   level or the rest is wasted.
3. Then prose: read aloud; the ear catches what the eye forgives.
4. Last pass, only dialogue tags, adverbs, and filter words ("seemed",
   "began to", "felt like") — the small lies that soften specificity.

## Across sessions

A story run spans sessions — drafts, notes, prior revisions. `remember`
the spine (premise, protagonist, want, change) when it settles; `recall`
before reopening a draft; `connect_memories` between the story premise
and the themes it serves so a later session can `expand_memory` from the
premise and find the craft notes.

## Ground rules

- No scene without change; no dialogue without intent.
- Cut more than you keep. The reader never misses what was never there.
- Specificity is the whole trick: the exact object, the exact gesture.
